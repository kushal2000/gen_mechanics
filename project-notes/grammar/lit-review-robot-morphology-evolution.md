# Literature review: evolutionary / co-design methods for robot morphology

Scope: robot hands and grippers, plus legged/locomotion and general modular robots, read against our own grammar-based hand evolution problem (population ~1,024, 40 generations, top-50% truncation, one offspring per survivor, single shared morphology-conditioned RL policy in Isaac Lab, noisy training-return fitness). Time-boxed web research (~50 min), no repository clones. Every claim is tagged **[verified]** (I opened the primary source — arXiv abstract/HTML/PDF or publisher page — and quote or closely paraphrase it) or **[snippet]** (I only saw a search-engine summary of the source and did not open it, or the fetch tool's extraction was itself a paraphrase of a paraphrase). Where a WebFetch summary looked like it might contain numbers invented by the small summarizing model rather than the paper, I've flagged that explicitly rather than repeating the number as fact.

**Reading on gripper/hand section:** the field is split between (a) pure **parametric co-design** with continuous/discrete/categorical parameters on a *fixed* topology, optimized by Bayesian opt/GA/gradient (Hardware-as-Policy, Function-based Parametric Co-Design, Yale GA lineage, Fin-Ray works) — none of these have a growth/shrink operator at all, because topology is fixed; and (b) a newer wave of **grammar/graph generators with a shared, morphology-conditioned policy** (House of Dextra, and by extension Lang2Morph which emits OPH-compatible grammars). Only House of Dextra couples an actual structural-generation grammar to a genuinely shared cross-embodiment RL policy at any real morphology-search scale — making it the closest published analogue to our setup, and its explicitly reported limitations (fixed 6-node topology, no removal operator, task-specificity, anthropomorphic-hand underperformance under the shared policy) are directly relevant.

## 4. Shared-controller-across-population comparison (explicit answer to coverage item 4)

This is the crux question for us: **when one controller must serve an evolving population, how do published methods stop the controller's familiarity with old designs from dominating selection of new ones?** Four data points found, ranked by how directly they answer it:

1. **Strgar & Kriegman, "Accelerated co-design of robots through morphological pretraining" (arXiv:2502.10862)** [verified]. This is the most load-bearing result in this review. They directly A/B test three regimes on the *same* voxel-robot + truncation-selection setup that closely resembles ours (pop 8192, top-50% truncation of parents+offspring pooled, 100–180 gens): (a) simultaneous co-design (controller and morphology evolve together, no pretraining) — **severe diversity collapse**; (b) zero-shot evolution with a frozen pretrained universal controller — **also collapses**, because the frozen controller still has systematic blind spots/preferences; (c) few-shot evolution — **reset the controller to its pretrained checkpoint at the start of every single generation, then fine-tune for a small fixed budget (60 gradient steps) before scoring that generation's population**. Only (c) preserves and even increases diversity while matching or beating (a)/(b) on performance. Mechanism: the reset removes the *cumulative* familiarity bias that a controller which keeps learning across generations would otherwise build up toward whatever the population currently looks like, while the short fine-tune still lets each new design get a fair, current evaluation rather than being judged purely on an out-of-distribution frozen policy's zero-shot competence.
2. **"Evolutionary Brain–Body Co-Optimization Consistently Fails to Select for Morphological Potential" (arXiv:2508.17464)** [snippet — title/thesis trusted, specific numbers not independently verified]. States as its central finding that shared/reused controllers cause selection to retain designs the controller already fits, masking designs with higher latent potential — independent confirmation, from a different research group/framing, of the same failure mode Strgar & Kriegman fix. Should be read in full to see whether their proposed mitigation overlaps with the reset-and-fine-tune recipe.
3. **DERL (Gupta et al., arXiv:2102.02202)** [snippet, not independently confirmed beyond abstract]. Each morphology in each generation is claimed (per general literature knowledge, not confirmed by my fetch) to get its own from-scratch-or-inherited RL training rather than sharing one frozen policy; the paper's "morphological Baldwin effect" finding is that evolution comes to favor morphologies that *learn* fast under fresh training, which is a related but distinct phenomenon from familiarity bias — it's a bias toward "quick learners," not toward "already-known designs." I was not able to verify the exact controller-sharing mechanics in this pass; flagged for full read.
4. **Transform2Act (arXiv:2110.03659)** [verified abstract only]. Sidesteps the shared-controller-bias question altogether by not having generations at all — morphology change is folded into the action space of one continuously-trained policy, so there is no "familiar vs novel design" selection step in the evolutionary-algorithm sense. This is a genuinely different paradigm (self-modifying single-lifetime agent) rather than a population-GA answer to our problem, but it demonstrates one clean way to avoid the bias: eliminate the population/generation boundary that lets a controller get "stuck" preferring old designs.
5. **House of Dextra (arXiv:2512.03743)** [verified]. Does not test resets or a fine-tuning schedule; instead avoids the failure mode partly by scale (2000–8000 hand variants seen by the shared policy per "family" before search even starts) and partly by only fine-tuning the *single final winning design* after search, not every generation's population. It does, however, self-report the resulting bias in a different form: its shared policy performs much better on radially-symmetric than anthropomorphic hands, i.e. the pretraining distribution itself, not a per-generation reset failure, produced a systematic preference.

**Bottom line for our project:** the Strgar & Kriegman reset-and-short-fine-tune recipe is the most directly transferable, concretely tested mitigation for controller familiarity bias found in this review, and it maps almost one-to-one onto our setup's population size and truncation-selection scheme. Neither of the two closest hand-specific works (House of Dextra, Function-based Parametric Co-Design) actually tests or discusses this failure mode with our level of rigor — House of Dextra avoids it by brute-force pretraining scale rather than by a per-generation reset protocol, and it still shows a form of it (anthropomorphic-hand underperformance).

## Synthesis mapped to our open questions

**Framing (per project owner).** Two different things are being asked of "balance," and the literature only ever really speaks to one or the other at a time, so we keep them apart throughout:

- **[GRAMMAR]** — what the grammar itself is responsible for: expressivity (can it reach the space of hands we want), simplicity/elegance (small rule set, few redundant ways to say the same design), and operator locality/reversibility (how far one edit moves the phenotype, and whether it can be undone). This is a property of the generator, independent of any particular evolutionary run.
- **[PROCESS]** — what the evolutionary/selection loop is responsible for: diversity preservation, preventing one founder/root hand's lineage from dominating the population (ancestry collapse), and preventing the run from degenerating late into small numeric refinements of whatever already won early (stagnation). This is a property of selection, population management, and the schedule of operator use — not of the grammar.
- **[CONTROLLER]** — fairness of evaluation under a single shared controller. Per the clarification received: this is a controller/process design problem (reset schedules, fine-tuning budgets, pretraining scale), not something the grammar can or should solve, even though a grammar-side mitigation (e.g. an insertion prior) can *reduce how often* the controller is asked to judge something wildly unfamiliar. We report shared-controller literature here but file it under controller/process, not grammar.

We also do not anchor on "40 generations" below — our own run happens to use that budget, but the literature comparisons and recommendations generalize to "however many evolutionary stages we end up running."

**[GRAMMAR] Expressive-vs-tractable balance and growth from minimal seeds.** Direct evidence is thin, and it splits along encoding type rather than along any explicit "expressivity vs. tractability" framing — nobody we found poses the trade-off in those terms. Most parametric hand co-design work (Function-based Parametric Co-Design, Hardware-as-Policy, Yale GA lineage) sidesteps the question entirely by fixing topology and only exposing continuous/discrete parameters: maximally tractable, minimally expressive, by construction. House of Dextra sits one step up in expressivity (a real grammar with discrete choices) but keeps a fixed 6-node topology and no removal rule — the authors self-report this as limiting exploration, i.e. they chose tractability over expressivity and know it. The classic generative-encoding line (Hornby & Pollack L-systems, Auerbach/Bongard CPPN, Cheney et al. multi-material CPPN) is the strongest precedent for a small, elegant rule set producing large expressive reach: compact genomes, grown via NEAT-style complexification from minimal seeds, explicitly credited with reaching good designs "more quickly ... particularly in the early stages of evolution" (Hornby & Pollack, per search summary). That is a grammar-level property (a small rule set that is inherently biased toward regular, reusable structure) rather than a process-level one, and it is the closest published analogue to what we want from our own grammar's simplicity/elegance. Strgar & Kriegman's balanced add/remove voxel mutation is the strongest evidence for a genuinely *symmetric* growth/shrink operator, but it is a direct (non-grammar) voxel encoding, so it speaks to operator balance in general, not to grammar elegance specifically.

**[PROCESS] Founder dominance (ancestry collapse) and late-stage stagnation, work by work.** The project owner asked us to state this explicitly per work rather than folding it into prose. Only a handful of the papers in Sections 1–4 run a real multi-generation population loop long enough for either failure mode to be observable; most others are structurally exempt (single-shot search, no generations) or simply didn't report on it.

| Work | Founder/ancestry dominance addressed? | Late-stage stagnation (collapse to small numeric refinement) addressed? |

**[PROCESS] Diversity maintenance more generally.** MAP-Elites/QD is the field's canonical general-purpose answer to both founder dominance and, by construction, an implicit form of stagnation resistance (an archive keeps refining many niches instead of over-exploiting one). We could not find a MAP-Elites-for-hand-morphology paper specifically; the closest match (graph-based QD soft grippers, arXiv:2609.20087) is worth a full read. Outside QD, the survey (arXiv:2512.04770) names novelty search, speciation, and archive-based population management as the standard toolbox — none of which is used by any hand-specific paper we found, and none of which our own pipeline currently uses either (we rely on truncation selection alone, i.e. the same starting condition Strgar & Kriegman showed collapses without their reset fix).

**[GRAMMAR] Duplicates / canonical forms.** Not directly addressed by name as a grammar-level property in anything we found. House of Dextra's "path-level tabu tracking" is a process-level, search-trajectory dedup (not a phenotype-canonicalization mechanism), and RoboGrammar's constrained grammar limits the space to guaranteed-valid designs but does not canonicalize or dedupe equivalent phenotypes. This looks like a genuine, under-addressed gap for grammar design specifically (see Gaps below).

**[GRAMMAR + PROCESS] Cost/parsimony pressure.** Explicit parsimony terms are rare in the hand-design literature specifically — House of Dextra, Function-based Parametric Co-Design, and Hardware-as-Policy all optimize pure task performance with no cost term, a real gap given how central cost/motor-count is to our own "halve motors at a proxy cost" experiment. The survey (arXiv:2512.04770) confirms parsimony pressure, cost constraints, and staged-growth strategies are the field's three standard bloat controls; a cost term can live either in the grammar (e.g. cost-weighted operator proposal probabilities, which is grammar-level) or in selection (a fitness penalty, which is process-level) — the literature doesn't consistently distinguish these either, so this is a place we may need to make our own choice rather than follow precedent.

**[CONTROLLER] Fairness of evaluation under a shared controller.** Filed here as a controller/process concern, not a grammar one, per the clarification. See Section 4 above for the full comparison; in short, Strgar & Kriegman's per-generation reset-to-pretrained-checkpoint + short fixed fine-tune is the most concretely tested, directly portable mitigation found, and it is complementary to (not a substitute for) any grammar-side insertion prior — the insertion prior shapes what gets *proposed*, the reset protocol shapes whether the shared controller *judges it fairly* once proposed.

**Noisy fitness under a small, fixed evaluation budget.** Two useful results, both process/evaluation-level rather than grammar-level: (1) Function-based Parametric Co-Design's TPE just accepts Bayesian optimization's native robustness to "a noisy, stochastic black-box function" rather than adding explicit averaging — a weaker answer than we'd want; (2) the computation-budget empirical study (arXiv:2409.08621) gives an actionable recipe that matches our situation: deliberately under-train/under-evaluate each candidate during search (accept more noise, more candidates), then retrain/re-evaluate only the final winning design at full budget — spend the scarce evaluation budget on breadth, invest depth once. It also found that per-candidate training budget itself biases which designs win (lower budgets bias toward simpler designs) — an independent, process-level source of distortion separate from controller-familiarity bias, worth tracking separately.

**Agreement/conflict with our own findings.** Our finding that neutral drift relaxes toward the growth prior agrees qualitatively with the generative-encoding literature's claim that compact generative encodings bias populations toward "regular, modular, reused" structure by construction (Hornby & Pollack) — this is a [GRAMMAR]-level effect in both cases: our insertion prior and a generative encoding's implicit bias are both properties of the generator, not of selection. Our finding that structural operators cause large (9–14mm) tip motion vs. small-step operators' local (0–4mm) effect is a [GRAMMAR]-level locality measurement with no direct quantitative analogue found anywhere in this review — nobody reports tip-displacement-per-operator-class numbers, which suggests we may be among the first to measure operator locality this explicitly. Our structural-early/small-step-late schedule finding is a [PROCESS]-level result (it's about *when* to deploy which operators, not what the grammar can express) that is directionally consistent with the generative-encoding intuition that growth-first regimes work better early, but we found nothing that tests such a schedule explicitly for hands, for grammars, or under a shared-controller regime — this may be a genuinely novel process-level contribution worth writing up on its own.

## Gaps nobody addressed (that we found)

Tagged [GRAMMAR] (encoding/operator design), [PROCESS] (selection/population management), or [CONTROLLER] (evaluation fairness) per the responsibility split above.

- **[GRAMMAR+PROCESS+CONTROLLER combined]** No paper combines all of: (a) a growth/shrink-*balanced* structural grammar for hands specifically, (b) a single shared controller across the whole evolving population, (c) an explicit per-generation reset/fine-tune or other controller-fairness protocol, and (d) a small, fixed evolutionary-stage budget. House of Dextra gets closest on (a)+(b) but not (c)/(d) at our scale; Strgar & Kriegman get (b)+(c)+(d)-adjacent but their encoding is voxels, not a kinematic grammar, and they don't build hands.
- **[GRAMMAR]** Duplicate/canonical-form detection in phenotype space (not just search-trajectory dedup) — not found addressed anywhere in this pass.
- **[GRAMMAR/PROCESS, unresolved split]** Cost/parsimony pressure jointly tuned against growth/shrink operator balance for hands specifically — the general-morphology survey names parsimony pressure as a standard tool, but no hand paper we found actually uses one, let alone studies whether it belongs in the proposal distribution (grammar) or the fitness function (selection), or its interaction with operator balance the way we have.
- **[GRAMMAR]** Quantified operator locality (tip-displacement per operator class, as we measure) — not found reported anywhere; may be a genuine gap our own experiments are filling.
- **[PROCESS]** QD/MAP-Elites specifically for kinematic hand-grammar morphology space, as a founder-dominance/stagnation defense — the closest hit (arXiv:2609.20087) is grippers-as-graphs, not a hand kinematic grammar, and wasn't confirmed to use RL control at all.
- **[PROCESS]** A late-stage-stagnation-specific schedule or detector (as opposed to founder-dominance/diversity-collapse, which at least Strgar & Kriegman and LOKI address) — nobody we found treats "the run degenerates into small numeric refinements late" as a distinct, separately-measured failure mode from early ancestry collapse; our own structural-early/small-step-late schedule finding may be one of the only concrete results on this specific question.
- **[GRAMMAR vs CONTROLLER, unresolved]** Explicit ablation of insertion-prior-style mutation biasing (grammar-side) versus controller-side reset/fine-tuning as competing or complementary bias-correction mechanisms — nobody we found tests both together, though our synthesis above argues they should be complementary rather than substitutes.

## 5 papers to read in full first

1. **Strgar & Kriegman, "Accelerated co-design of robots through morphological pretraining"** — [arXiv:2502.10862](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10862).
