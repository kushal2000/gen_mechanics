# The hand grammar (October 2026)

**This is the current, up-to-date grammar**: branch `hand-grammar`, latest label the tag `hand-grammar-2026-10-20` (moved from `martin/hand-grammar` on 2026-10-16). Since 2026-10-23 every grammar hand can also be generated as printed, screw-assembled parts (step 19); that work and the grammar changes it needed are on branch `hand-build`, not yet merged.

Evolve to generalize trains one joint-token transformer across many hands and lets evolution choose which hands it trains on. The grammar defines the space of hands evolution searches. It has to do three things: express the commercial hands we train or test on (their structure, roughly in place), narrow down to hands evolution can search and we can build (the Feetech rules), and produce hands that all load into one simulator layout for one shared policy.

Since 2026-10-14 the grammar is built around one motor, the Feetech HL-3915 servo. The rebuild followed Martin's notes of 10/13, the Feetech study (`experiments/E15_feetech_buildability/summary.md`) and an independent review (`experiments/E16_grammar_rebuild/summary.md`). Every decision, with its reason, is in `GRAMMAR-LOCK-2026-10-07.md` (revision of 2026-10-14). It combines three lines of work: ours on `martin/hand-grammar`, Vatsal's physical grammar (the hull palm, the finger mount, joint kinds, building each finger clear of the others) and Kushal's uniform commercial hands (shared dynamics, the joint sign convention).

This document describes the code in `hand_sampler/grammar/` and is updated with every change to the grammar.

## Three layers

1. **The grammar:** what a hand can be.
2. **The rules:** limits that random generation and every mutation obey by construction, so no hand is generated and then thrown away for breaking a rule. Evolution and the simulator use the **Feetech rules**: hands buildable from Feetech servos. In the viewer: Feetech Rules, No Rules (the whole grammar) or Custom Rules.
3. **Viability checks:** the two properties of a whole hand that no rule can guarantee: its parts keep clear of each other (C1), and its fingertips can meet above the palm (C2).

## How a hand is built, step by step

### What a hand stores

Only these numbers, all whole: millimetres in steps of 10, degrees in steps of 30.
- **Per finger:** its base position (y, z) on the palm, its facing, its tilt, and for each joint: its type (hinge, coupled or sliding), its axis (two angles) and the length of the link after it.
- **Per palm cut (at most one):** its direction and its distance from the palm centre.

That is 4 numbers per finger, 3 per joint and 2 for a cut, plus each joint's type. Random Feetech hands have 14-98 numbers (mean 51); the commercial fits range from 14 (Dex1) to 85 (SHARPA, Shadow). Everything in steps 8-13 is computed from these numbers and the motor.

**The search space** (`hand_sampler/grammar/space.py`, counted exactly; `experiments/E16_grammar_rebuild/ours/search_space.py`). Hands of 2-6 fingers each stage allows (step 16), before the centre rule (step 10) and the clearance check; a palm cut multiplies each by at most 73 more. Search-space numbers always count every finger count (Martin, 10/20):

| Stage | Hands (2-6 fingers) | Turns and mirror images counted once (step 16) | Usable: mount at the centre, pass C1 and C2 (3,000 uniform samples) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coarse: bases every 40 mm, the three kinds, links in 20 mm steps (where random hands start) | 10^42.8 (10^37.1 before 11/04) | about 10^42 | (the shares measured 10/20 at 10^38.3: 65% centred; 2 of 3,000 pass C1 and C2) |
| Fine: bases every 10 mm, 31 axis directions, 10 mm steps | 10^76.5 (10^63.1 before 11/04; 10^70.2 with bases every 40 mm) | about 10^76 | (measured 10/20: 67% centred; none of 3,000 pass C1) |
| The grammar's own limits (No Rules) | 10^123 | | |

On 10/23 the counts fell from 10^38.3 and 10^65.4: a crossing's first servo now always lies along the finger, and the link before a crossing must fit the servo after it (step 5). On 11/04 they rose (step 5, step 3): floors at the next whole mm instead of up to the 10 mm grid, the length from which a servo lies along its link, side mounts, crossings into a twist and flipped spreads (coarse 10^42.8, fine 10^70.2), then the fine stage's bases every 10 mm (fine 10^76.5). Per finger count, coarse: 2 fingers 10^15.0, 3 10^22.2, 4 10^29.2, 5 10^36.1, 6 10^42.8; fine: 10^26.2, 10^38.9, 10^51.5, 10^64.0, 10^76.5. The usable shares (last column) are from 10/20 and were not measured again.

Six-finger hands are about 99% of each total. Earlier counts were of four-finger hands only: 10^29.7 on the three kinds and 10^44.2 with all axes (10/16), about 10^50 before the rebuild. Most of the count is five-joint fingers with long links, which collide more often; the random draw (step 5) picks joint counts uniformly instead. Ways to cut the space further are listed, none adopted, in `search-space-options.md`.

### Building a random hand

**1. The origin and the mount.**
- The origin (0, 0) is the centre of the palm, where the hand mounts. The mount is a column 40 mm across and 40 mm deep behind the palm's back face. It attaches the hand to the arm, or to the world in our palm-up task. Its far end is the **board bay** (Martin, 10/23: the Palm Hub board behind the palm): a box 76 x 60 mm and 22 mm deep, whose floor bolts straight to the arm's flange, turned in 15 degree steps and shifted up to 15 mm per hand to keep clear of the base links (`derive.hand_geometry`). **The mount and the bay are drawn and simulated, but have no say in a hand's fit** (Martin, 11/04: "the wrist mount and board bay should exist but should NOT have any impact on the fitting procedure"): they no longer count in the clearance check (step 14) or in where a base may go. The printed hand's back mount is that column; the board lies on the palm's back where it finds room (step 19).
- Axes: **x** points out of the palm on the grasp side; **y** and **z** lie in the palm. Nothing distinguishes "toward the fingers": fingers can go all round the centre.
- The palm is a flat plate 24 mm thick (step 10), centred on x = 0: its faces are at x = -12 and +12 mm.
- The mount disc is always part of the palm, so it is the smallest palm. It replaces the old 20 mm wrist (heel) disc, which had no physical reason; this one is where the hand is held.
- **Why (Martin, 10/13):** the hand mounts at its back, not at a wrist on one side, so its base is the middle of the palm.
- **The mount sits at the palm's centre** (Martin, 10/16): the centroid of the hull of the base servos' housings lies within 20 mm of it, half the coarse stage's base step, in every stage (so every coarse hand stays a fine one; until 11/04 the rule was half the rules' own step, 5 mm for the commercial fits). Without this rule, moving every base by one grid step gave the same palm and fingers with the mount elsewhere: 13% of random hands had such a twin. Moving every base by d moves that centroid by exactly d, so at most one placement passes and no two hands differ only in where the mount sits. Random draws and mutations move a hand by the fewest grid steps that bring it within 20 mm (`operators.recentre`; on the coarse grid that is the one placement within half a step, so at most one placement passes there; on the fine grid a small change leaves the hand where it is); commercial fits move the palm frame back with it, so their errors are unchanged. The exact count of the search space leaves the rule out; 42% of uniformly drawn four-finger hands keep it.

**2. How many fingers:** uniform 2-6, and the hand keeps that count. If a finger cannot be placed (step 6), the whole hand starts again with the same count. Two because a single Wuji finger cannot learn the task (Kushal); six because the simulator has six finger slots.

**3. Where each finger's base goes.** In the coarse stage bases sit on a 40 mm grid, 40-90 mm from the centre: 20 spots, in rows of up to five (drawn below). The fine stage puts them every 10 mm, 208 spots, as the commercial fits do (Martin, 11/04: "stick to the current grid for the coarse, and then the 'fine' stage can have its values adjusted to allow good matching to commercial hands"; LEAP's fingers stand 45.4 mm apart).

```
   z
   ^     .   o   o   o   .        o  a spot: (y, z) in steps of 40 mm, 40-90 mm from the centre
   |     o   o   o   o   o           (rings at 40, 56.6, 80 and 89.4 mm)
   |     o   o   M   o   o        M  the mount, at the centre (not a spot)
   |     o   o   o   o   o        .  113 mm out: too far
   |     .   o   o   o   .
   +-----------------------> y    (y and z: -80, -40, 0, 40, 80 mm)
```

- A finger's base is drawn together with its facing, tilt and first joint's kind (steps 4 and 5): from all the combinations whose first motor clears the motors already placed by one printed wall (2 mm; the mount, until 11/04). It is drawn from what fits, not drawn and then rejected. Each combination is weighted so that first joints keep the kind mix (69/27/4) although a bend first has one tilt and the others four.
- There is no spacing rule and no rim rule. One rule (motors may not overlap the mount, each other or the palm; step 14) replaces the old 10 mm grid, the 19 mm spacing and the rim rule.
- **Why 40 mm:** with 35 mm, neighbouring first motors collide in 57% of the pairs of directions they can take; with 40 mm, 28%. No spacing short of 67 mm is safe for every pair, so the overlap rule is needed anyway, and 40 mm leaves it far more choices.
- **Why 40-90 mm:** an inner radius of one grid step keeps rows of five fingers (a 55 mm inner radius, which a larger mount would need, allows rows of three). 90 mm keeps the palm compact; the old 10-160 mm ring made palms twice the commercial size.

**4. Which way each finger points.**
- **Facing:** the direction the finger leaves its base, in the palm's plane, in 30 degree steps (0 points along +z, 90 along +y). Under the Feetech rules a finger faces outward: it makes at most 90 degrees with the line from the centre to its base, so it never points back across the palm. Tangential is allowed.
- **Tilt:** out of the plate, toward the grasp side: 0, 30, 60 or 90 degrees. +90 points straight up from the palm (DClaw). No finger points under the palm (Martin, 10/16; the range began at -30 for Barrett's thumb at -25, and the commercial fits now do without it). **A finger whose first joint is a bend has tilt 0** (Martin, 10/16): the tilt turns the finger about its bend axis, which lies in the plate whatever the tilt, so a tilted bend-first finger is the same chain as an untilted one with its first bend's zero moved by the tilt (only its base servo would sit differently). Commercial fits fold such a tilt into that joint's zero-pose offset.
- **Why outward (Martin, 10/13):** a finger pointing back across the palm runs over the other fingers' bases and the object's place.

**5. Each finger's joints and links,** from the base out.
- **Joints:** uniform 1-5. Joint j sits at the start of link j; the last link ends at the fingertip. Under the Feetech rules every joint is a hinge driven by its own servo.
- **Axis:** two angles in the link's frame (step 11). Each joint starts exactly on a kind, drawn with the commercial mix: bend 69%, spread 27%, twist 4%. Angled axes come only from later mutations; the grid has 31 axis directions.
- **Link lengths:** each link is drawn uniformly among the lengths it may take, given the joints at its two ends (the earlier servo lying along its link, unless the row says otherwise):

| Earlier joint, then next | bend | spread | twist |
|---|---|---|---|
| bend | 27-90 (27-41 turn the next servo; from 42 it lies) | 0-40 side-mounted, or 42-90 | 0-30 side-mounted, or 32-90 |
| bend (turned) | 34-90 (34-48 turn the next servo; from 49 it lies) | 0-40 side-mounted, or 49-90 | 0-30 side-mounted, or 39-90 |
| spread | 0-40 side-mounted, or 41-90 | 30-90 (30-40 flip the next servo; from 41 it lies) | 0-30 side-mounted, or 31-90 |
| spread (flipped) | 0-40 side-mounted, or 52-90 | 41-90 (41-51 flip the next servo; from 52 it lies) | 0-30 side-mounted, or 42-90 |
| twist | 28-90 (28-41 turn the next servo; from 42 it lies) | 31-90 (31-41 flip the next servo; from 42 it lies) | 32-90 |

  The lengths run in 10 mm steps from the shortest, with the length from which the next servo lies along the link (42 mm, off the grid) added. The shortest keeps the next servo's back clear of this servo's swinging front, plus a wall (E15's rule), to the next whole millimetre past the build clearance (Martin, 11/04: with floors raised to the 10 mm grid, a 40.3 mm floor became 50, and LEAP and Allegro, built from servos of this size, fitted only scaled up; until then a floor went up to the grid, or 1 mm past it where the grid left less than 1 mm, Martin 10/23). A servo lies along the finger, its body behind its axis. **A bend servo turns only when the link before it is too short for that** (Martin, 10/16): turned 90 degrees about its axis, its body on the back of the finger, only its 20 mm width lies along the link (10 mm behind its axis, against 24.5). The turned body then stands 24.5 mm out behind the finger (its link is 36.5 mm deep, not 24), and at the bend's -30 degree hyperextension its back corner swings 20.9 mm forward (13.8 for a servo lying along the link), so the link after it needs more (the turned row). **A spread servo flips there instead** (11/04, as LEAP's thumb servos sit; it cannot turn, its back would point along its axis): it lies along the link after its joint, its body in that link with its back toward the fingertip, and the link before holds its horns in a yoke round its front. **Where the links on both sides have room, a bend servo is flipped instead of turned** (Martin, 10/29: turned, it stands 24.5 mm out on the finger's back; "these motors could have been rotated 90 degrees to allow the finger to be thinner ... the output of the MCP DOF connects to the output of the PIP DOF"). A link before a flipped servo carries two yokes back to back: 31 mm after a bend or a spread (30 between spreads), 32 after a twist, 38 after a turned bend. The link after holds the flipped body: 42 mm before another flipped bend (41 between spreads), 52 before a lying servo, and a fingertip 49 (`motor.pose_floor_mm`). Of the ways a finger's bend servos can sit with room in every link, the one with the fewest turned, then the fewest flipped, then with the turned ones nearest the palm (`hand.servo_poses`); a spread past a short link is always flipped (`hand.flipped_spread`). Turning a bend and flipping a spread set the floors. A servo next to a crossing, and every servo at the base, lies along the finger. The ranges are unchanged. Over all 31 axes the shortest is 27-61 mm (a side mount aside), a fingertip 38-53. The fingertip link is at least 38 mm (39 after a twist), 45 after a turned bend, 49 after a flipped servo: the last servo's swing, a wall and a 22 mm pad, room for the printed fingertip's whole flare and dome (Martin, 11/02: every fingertip the same cylinder and dome; since 11/03 it is 28 mm across, which needs a 17 mm pad; the pad stays at 22). Under the Feetech rules a whole finger is at most 225 mm (Martin, 10/16); five joints on the three kinds need at most 204 mm at their shortest (bends and spreads in turn, 42 + 41 + 42 + 41 + 38, none side-mounted; five bends 174: 27 + 34 + 34 + 34 + a 45 mm fingertip), so every five-joint finger fits.
- **Two joints at one point (a 0 mm link)** are allowed when the first axis is square to the finger and the second is square to it too (a bend and a spread, or axes between them) or runs along it (a twist; 11/04: the base of LEAP's thumb, whose twist servo stands across the first servo's axis), never two in a row, never with a twist first (its servo cannot slide aside). The earlier servo then slides along its own axis, away from the joint, by 28 mm (square axes, or a bend into a twist) to 42.5 mm (a spread into a twist; 35.6 for axes 30 degrees apart): far enough that a plane square to its axis keeps the two servos a wall apart over the whole range, plus 1.5 mm for the horn's centre screw head between them (Martin, 10/23). **Side mounts** (11/04): such a pair may also sit nearer than the next servo's lying floor, 10-40 mm apart on the grid (10-30 into a twist), the earlier servo slid as at 0 mm (`hand.crossing_link`): LEAP's spread servo sits 15 mm from the next bend's. A spread crossing a bend at the base puts the spread servo 32.5 mm behind the palm. Both servos of a crossing lie along the finger (the first is never turned or flipped), and the link before a crossing must also fit the crossing's second servo, which sits in the next part: 45 mm after a bend, 44 after a spread, 52 after a turned bend, less a side mount's offset (`motor.crossing_floor_mm`; found building the parts, 10/23). A 2-2-1 thumb (spread-bend-spread-bend-bend) is possible, and LEAP's (a bend into a twist at 0 mm, then two flipped spreads: links 0, 31, 41 and a 60 mm fingertip).
- **No angles between links:** at the zero pose a finger is a straight line and bends only through its joints.
- **Why:** Martin's notes 2.11 (hinges only, crossings anywhere, buildable by construction) and E15.

**6. The clearance test.** After each finger is drawn, the whole hand so far must pass C1 (step 14) at the zero pose (also the start pose since the fingers start open). If it fails, that finger's base, direction and joints are drawn again, up to 50 times; after that the whole hand starts again with the same finger count, up to 20 times, then generation reports that no such hand exists. **Why:** Vatsal's method (each finger clear of the others as it is placed), and Martin's note 2.12 (never silently fewer fingers).

**7. An optional palm cut.** On half the hands (when the rules allow a palm joint), the palm is cut along a straight line and the part beyond the line hinges on the rest, +/-30 degrees. The cut is drawn uniformly among the lines (30 degree directions, 10 mm offsets) that leave at least one finger on each side, keep the mount whole on the fixed part (at least 30 mm from the centre) and keep the hand clear. Every finger beyond the line rides on the moving part. **Why:** Martin's note 2.13 (palm joints decided on the finished palm; several fingers on one part, like a pinkie and a ring finger); a straight cut of a convex palm leaves two convex plates. If no cut fits, the hand is drawn again, so half the hands have one (48% of 1000).

### What is computed from the stored numbers

**8. The motor and how deep each base sits.** Every joint is the same servo: a 34 x 20 x 29 mm box, its axis along the 29 mm edges, 9.5 mm from one end and centred in the 20 mm width (`motor.py`). It lies along the finger, its body behind its axis, except a bend servo whose link before it is too short for that, which is flipped to lie along the link after it or, where that has no room, turned with its body on the back of the finger, and a spread servo there, which is flipped (step 5). Its body sits in the link before its joint (the palm, for a finger's first joint), and the link after it holds the horn and the idler in a yoke; a flipped servo's the other way round. A finger's base joint sits below the palm's front face, just deep enough that the housing of its first servo (and of the second, when the two share the point) is flush with the face: 12 mm for a bend first, 16.5 for a spread, 11.5 for a twist (flat fingers). **Why (Martin, 10/14):** the base joint sits half a servo plus a wall from the face, so the fingers start as close to the working side of the palm as the servo allows.

**9. Solids.** Every solid the clearance check and the simulator see:
- each link but the last: one box, square to its frame, around what it holds at its two ends: its yoke (a flipped servo's body), and the next servo's body (the yoke on a flipped one). A bending link is 33 mm wide and 24 mm deep (36.5 mm when a servo it holds is turned) and runs from 12 mm behind its joint to the next servo's front: links are servo-sized;
- the fingertip (each finger's last link): the convex hull of a capsule along the link, radius 14 mm (Martin, 11/03: 28 mm, the 33 mm fingertip "too thick"; until then 16.5, half the yoke's 33 mm width along the last servo's axis), from the yoke's back (12 mm behind its joint) to the fingertip, and of the yoke's box (after a flipped servo the fingertip holds its body: the box covers its front and horns, the capsule the rest) (Martin, 10/23: the printed bracket on the last servo sticks out of the capsule). **Why (Martin, 10/16):** a rounded fingertip, a little thicker than the yoke's 24 mm depth. The simulator's collider, the clearance check and the viewer's mesh all use the same 58 points (the capsule's 50 and the yoke's 8; at most 45 vertices and 61 faces, inside PhysX's 64-vertex, 64-face GPU limit);
- each finger's base servo, fixed to its plate;
- the main plate, the part beyond a cut, the mount, the board bay, and the cut's hinge servo.

Mass comes from a density of 1750 kg/m^3 over each box and capsule: a finger weighs 344 g at the median (up to 862 g).

**10. Palm plates.** The palm's outline is the convex hull of the mount disc, every base servo's housing seen from above, and the hinge servo's housing, at most 32 corners. Every servo sits inside the palm by construction; there is no other palm parameter. The plate is 24 mm thick: a flat bend servo in its housing. Servos that stand deeper stick out behind: a spread servo 9 mm, a twist servo 14 mm, a crossed spread 31 mm; in 73% of random hands something sticks out behind (median 14 mm, at most 43). Behind the palm is free in our palm-up task.

A cut splits the outline into two convex plates, each stopping 4.3 mm short of the line: an 8.6 mm seam, because a 24 mm plate cannot swing +/-30 degrees through a zero-width cut. The hinge axis runs along the line in the plate's mid-plane. Its servo sits on the main palm's side at the 10 mm spot along the line nearest the middle of the chord that clears every base servo. Random palms measure 177 cm^2 at the median (commercial hands 97 cm^2 at their real size, 196 cm^2 at the scale Feetech servos build them, under the same outline rule).

**11. Each finger's frame,** at the zero pose: **x** along the finger (from facing and tilt); **y**, the closing direction, the palm's normal turned by the tilt; **z** = x cross y, the bend axis, in the plate. The frame depends on the finger alone. Joint axes are written in it; links are straight, so every link of a finger shares it at rest. **Why (Martin, 10/13):** + on every bend then moves the fingertip toward the palm's working face, the same way on every finger. (From 10/07 to 10/13 the closing direction pointed at the object's start point; it made a finger's frame depend on the other fingers.)

**12. Each joint's kind, sign and range.** The kind is whichever of the frame's z, y or x the axis lies closest to; it is a label, not a stored choice.

| Kind | Axis near | + means | Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| bend | z | the fingertip moves toward the closing direction (the palm's working side) | -30 to +90 deg |
| spread | y | the fingertip moves toward +y of the palm (if it cannot, +z, then +x) | +/-30 deg |
| twist | x | a right-handed turn about the link | +/-90 deg |
| palm joint | the cut's line | the part beyond the cut rises toward the grasp side | +/-30 deg |
| sliding | the axis | along the axis | -20 to +25 mm |

- A coupled joint (No Rules only) keeps its own kind and sign, follows the joint before it at 1.1 times its angle, and takes 1.1 times that joint's range.
- **Why:** computed signs make + mean the same on every hand (Kushal's convention); one range per kind; 1.1 is the typical commercial coupling.

**13. Poses and the object start.** At the zero pose every joint is at 0 and every finger is straight. Episodes start from the zero pose: the fingers start open (Martin, 10/20; until then every bend started at 0.35 of its upper limit, which stood curled fingers over the palm). The object starts over the centroid of the palm's outline, at the lowest height (from 5 mm above the front face, in 1 mm steps) where it clears every solid by 5 mm at the start pose. It never starts inside the hand.

### Checking, changing and simulating

**14. Viability checks.**
- **C1, clearance:** every two solids that do not touch by construction keep one printed wall (2 mm) apart, at the zero pose, which is now also the start pose (exact box distances). The mount and the board bay are not checked (step 1). Touching by construction: the main plate with the hinge servo and the part beyond the cut; a plate with its base servos (they sit in pockets); within a finger, the two sides of each joint (and, across a crossing or a side mount, the links on either side). One exception keeps a link from lying in the palm: at the zero pose a plate is checked against the far end of a link hinged to it. A base servo's housing must also stay out of a cut's seam. Collisions while the fingers move are left to the physics and the policy (Martin, 10/14). Generation keeps C1 (step 6).
- **C2, fingertips can meet:** each fingertip is sampled 1,500 times over its own joints' ranges (the palm joint included, one hinge angle shared by every finger beyond the cut); a sample counts if it is above the plate and over the palm (the outline grown by 20 mm); a hand passes if some pair of fingers' samples come within 20 mm of each other. Every commercial hand passes.
- Of random Feetech hands, 73% pass both (C1 100%, C2 73%): 1.37 draws per usable hand, 0.1 s each (1000 hands, re-measured 2026-10-16 after the day's rule changes).
- A physics grasp test (C5) is deferred until the grammar work is done.
- **The bay** (10/23 to 11/04): a hand failed C1 when no turn and shift of the board bay kept a wall from every finger's base links over their ranges (3-7% of random hands). Since 11/04 the mount and the bay have no say in a hand's fit (step 1).

**15. Rule sets.** The grammar's own limits (No Rules) allow hinge, coupled and sliding joints, links of 0 or 10-200 mm, fingers up to 500 mm, bases anywhere on a 10 mm grid 10-370 mm from the centre facing any way. These limits are the commercial hands' extremes at the scale Feetech servos build them (step 18) x 1.1, rounded out: the longest link (SHARPA at 3.1 x, 180 mm), the longest finger (SHARPA, 450 mm) and the farthest base (SVH at 3.05 x, 329 mm), set on 10/14. Since the rebuild of 10/16 the scaled hands reach less far (140 mm, 370 mm, 161 mm, SHARPA no longer scaled), so these limits are wider than the commercial hands need; kept for now (open). The **Feetech rules** (`FEETECH_RULES`, also `EVOLUTION_RULES`; the fine stage, with `FEETECH_COARSE_RULES` the coarse stage, step 16) are what evolution and the simulator use: hinges only; bases 40-90 mm out, every 10 mm in the fine stage and 40 in the coarse, facing outward; links at least the servo floors (step 5). Custom Rules can only tighten the grammar: joint types, maximum fingers (6), joints per finger (5), palm joints (1), finger length (Feetech 225 mm), link length (Feetech 90 mm), base grid and distance, outward facing, servo link lengths. The search space (2-6 fingers, before the clearance check): 10^42.8 hands in the coarse stage, 10^76.5 in the fine stage, 10^123 under the grammar's own limits. No Rules also allows every servo length off the grid (a floor, and where a servo lies along its link: 27, 42 mm), so every Feetech hand is inside the grammar's own limits. Random hands and every mutation keep the rules by construction.

**16. Mutation, in two stages** (Martin, 10/20). Random hands are drawn, and evolve first, in the **coarse stage** (`FEETECH_COARSE_RULES`): joints only on the three kinds (an axis turns 90 degrees, from one kind to another) and links at their shortest, then in 20 mm steps. The **fine stage** (`FEETECH_RULES`) then refines them: 31 axis directions (an axis turns or tips 30 degrees) and 10 mm steps. Every coarse hand is a fine hand, so the switch needs no conversion (`evolution/driver.py --rules coarse --fine-from-generation G`). A base moves one grid step (40 mm in the coarse stage, 10 in the fine), a facing or tilt 30 degrees.
- **Turns and mirror images are one hand** (Martin, 10/20): the palm-up task cannot tell apart a hand turned 90 degrees about the palm normal or mirrored, so random draws and mutations keep one copy of the eight (`operators.canonical`: the copy whose sorted fingers come first, among those that keep the rules and C1; a servo slid by two joints at one point keeps its side, so a mirror image is checked again). Its fingers are kept sorted.
- **Parameter operators:** move a finger's base (y or z); turn its facing; tilt it; change a link to its next allowed length (from its shortest down to 0 where two joints may share a point); turn or tip a joint's axis (links whose shortest length changes move to it); move the cut 10 mm; turn the cut 30 degrees.
- **Structural operators:** add a finger at a free spot, pointing away from the centre, tilt 0, with as many joints as the hand's fingers have on average (rounded up; Martin, 10/16), its joints and links drawn as for a random hand; remove a finger (a cut left with no finger on a side goes too); split a link to add a joint (at any 10 mm point on it; links too short for their new joints grow to their shortest); merge two links to remove a joint (at most 90 mm); couple or uncouple a joint (No Rules only); cut the palm; remove the cut.
- **The mount stays at the palm's centre:** a child whose palm centre moves more than half a base step from the mount is moved on the grid to centre it (`operators.recentre`); if that puts a base outside the ring, that option is not offered. So a hand can lose any of 78% of its fingers in one step (every hand can lose some finger), where before it could lose any.
- **One mutation:** an operator chosen at random among those that can act; its options shuffled (a split's new joint kind weighted by the commercial mix); the first child that keeps the rules and C1 is returned (Vatsal's method). From a usable hand, 94% of calls return a child and 96% of children are usable.

- **The scale:** tried at 1, 1.25, ..., 2.5, 3, ..., 5, then in 0.05 steps below the first that works. A thumb lying along the palm comes out standing off it.
- **Judged by the fingertip** (Martin, 10/20: "maybe doing it in fingertip manipulability space rather than joint space?"). **19. Building a hand** (Martin, 10/23: "hit Generate full design, and get a full 3D printable and screw-assemblable model"; `hand_sampler/cad/`, the viewer's Build folder, `python -m hand_sampler.cad --hand hand.json`). The grammar drives and OpenSCAD builds: Python writes the hand's frames (from `derive.fk`, `finger_geometry`, `hand_geometry`; no new kinematics) into a readable `hand.scad` over a fixed library (`hand_sampler/cad/scad/feetech_hand.scad`), whose build settings (fits, walls, arm thicknesses, flange angles) can be edited and re-rendered. The servo is measured from Feetech's STEP: a 34 x 20 x 23 mm case, 16 mm horns on both sides whose faces stand 29 mm apart (the datasheet and the STEP; Martin's servos measure 28.8-29, 11/04: the 30 mm he gave on 11/03 took in the centre screws' heads), 8 M2 holes on a 12 mm circle in each horn, 4 M2 holes on each horn face of the case, a 3-pin 1.25 mm socket on each 34 x 23 face.
- **A joint:** the servo's back end slides into a socket on the part before the joint (Martin, 10/24: an alignment feature, not the screws alone, and no more material than that and the screws): 0.1 mm a side, 1.2 mm walls on its 34 x 23 faces, 2 mm plates on its horn faces as far as the bosses of the 4 M2 x 3.5 screws through the case's far holes, and a back plate; the rest of the case is bare, and nothing passes round or under a horn (Martin, 10/26: there is no room there; the full-face plates of 10/24 are gone) (a crossing's second servo gets a closed housing). A servo whose idler horn is off (a twist: one arm on its output horn) is held by its idler face instead, in a flange cup: a 2 mm plate with that face's four holes (near and far) and the stub's, and a 1.2 mm rim 4 mm along the case for alignment; it slides in along its axis and is screwed through the holes in the previous U's bridge before that link goes on. The part after the joint carries a yoke: a U (a 3 mm arm on each horn, 29 mm apart inside, snug on the horns' faces (Martin, 11/04), each with 4 x M2 x 5 (Martin, 11/03: "one side is thicker than the other"; the idler's arm was 2 mm, held by the servo's 3.5 mm box screws): every horn takes four of its eight (Martin, 10/26): the usual square, else the other square, else the four nearest the usual, spread round the horn, where a connector window or plug of a servo the same part holds, or the next link swinging past a crossing's plate, would cut into a hole (Martin, 11/02: a hole cut open by a cable's window); a bridge as wide as the arms' discs just past the case's swing); one arm on the driven (output) horn for an axis leaning 30 degrees or more along the finger (a twist: a cantilever is fine, Martin, 10/26; the double-supported wrap of 10/24 was dropped), the idler horn (the one nearer the plugs) taken off; that arm and the link's connector are one plate from the horn's face, at least 6 mm thick; a plate on the driven horn for a crossing. Every part's outer edges are rounded 0.8 mm on its body as a whole (Martin, 10/28: rounding each piece on its own left fillets in the middle of faces wherever a gusset or a connector ran on from it): a part's pieces are built sharp, each reaching 1.2 mm into the pieces it joins (a connector's sides start exactly on the faces it joins and its reach is a smaller copy of its end inside them: reaching in at full size, it stood up to 0.25 mm off the arms' faces, a step at each seam; Martin, 11/02), and their union is shrunk by a 0.8 mm ball and grown back (a morphological opening, with manifold3d; a link's union is first grown by a 2 mm ball and shrunk back, a closing, which fillets its inside corners 2 mm, where the arms join the bridge, a gusset, a face plate or the connector, before its reliefs are cut (Martin, 11/02: fillets there); a fillet fills a square corner 0.83 mm out from the pieces, inside the 1 mm the part before the joint keeps from them, where 4 mm fillets cut into it; the opening cannot round outside edges more than 0.8 mm, since it removes anything thinner than twice its radius and the arms are 3 mm; the union's sliver facets merged first wherever that leaves the solid as it was, which makes it seconds, not minutes, once a link's reliefs are cut), which rounds every outer edge, leaves inside corners sharp and drops slivers under 1.6 mm; the palm's corners keep their 3 mm round. A link's reliefs (what it gives way to as the joints turn: its servo's case swept, its child swept, a crossing's relief) are cut before the rounding, so their edges come out round too (Martin, 11/02, of a crossing's relief: sharp edges to smooth); the servos' keep-outs, screw holes and screwdrivers' ways are cut after it, exact. OpenSCAD reads each rounded body from `bodies/` next to `hand.scad` (`ROUNDED = false` builds the bodies sharp there, after a setting they depend on changes). Gussets stiffen the L pieces' inside corners (Martin, 10/26): 45-degree triangles 16 mm a leg in the layers beside the servo, where the corner is open (the case fills it in between): in a socket's side-plate layers, between the connector's plate and the socket's front, where the connector lands on its side (there at most about 14.9 mm, so the 45-degree face clears the horn's keyhole); in a U's arm layers, between each arm's edge and the connector's back face, only where that face stands out past the bridge and never past it (Martin, 11/02: beside a bridge as tall as the connector's end they stood a millimetre proud of the arms, a step at each end), each one piece with its arm's disc, so its face runs on from the disc at a tangent (11/02: a 45-degree face met the disc's round end in a notch); where a gusset stops at the connector's edge, its face meets the connector's in an 8 mm arc tangent to both, so the arm runs on into the connector without an angle (Martin, 11/02: "these sharp angles on the horn mounting part that connect with the rest of the link should be smoothed with fillets"). None round a flange cup (Martin, 10/28, of the twist's holder: "what is this"): there they lay along the servo's sides with nothing to hold, and only read as ears. Each triangle is its own convex piece (a sweep hulls each piece), reaches 1.2 mm into both pieces it joins (so it is one solid with them), is kept to where the connector is, and is cut back like the rest of the part where something turns or must stay clear. The yoke slides on with the horns' centre screws out, which then go back through 5.6 mm holes in the arms (their heads are 5 mm across; Martin, 11/04: they did not fit the 5 mm holes).

Where something stands or turns behind the palm across it (a crossing's servo slid out behind, a pocket open through the back, a finger that goes in from behind with its servo), it stays if its inserts keep 1.5 mm and half of it is left, else moves to the nearest place clear of all that, wholly on its palm part (a note), else its inserts go into the palm's back face (a note); of 60 random hands, 57 have a column (14 of them with a screwdriver's way through it) and 3 the inserts in the back face, without material round them (the note says so). It finds room on the startup hand and on 2 of 60 random hands (6 of 60 with the fine stage's bases still every 40 mm): the servos stand round the palm's edge and the column takes its middle. Elsewhere it floats on its cables (a note). The overhang check counts the column as part of the simulator's palm.

### 5. Structural mutations

| Mutation | Effect |
|---|---|
| Add a finger | a finger at a free spot, pointing away from the centre, tilt 0, with as many joints as the hand's fingers have on average (rounded up; Martin, 10/16), its joints and links drawn as for a random hand |
| Remove a finger | a finger in one step, unless centring the rest puts a base outside the ring (78% of fingers can go); a cut left with no finger on a side goes too |
| Split a link | adds a joint at any 10 mm point on it; links grow to their shortest if needed |
| Merge two links | removes a joint; the merged link is at most 90 mm |
| Couple / uncouple a joint | No Rules only |
| Cut the palm / remove the cut | a straight cut with a finger on each side, or none |

## Not settled yet

- **The object start** is set aside (Martin, 10/20): the fingers start open, and the object starts just clear of them.
- **Torque.** The torque limit is now the HL-3915's stall torque, 1.39 N m: no random Feetech finger is too heavy to hold flat (the heaviest needs 0.71 N m; 8% need more than the old 0.5 N m), nor any commercial hand at its scale (at most 1.04 N m, Wuji v2 right). The servo's rated load is 0.44 N m, so stall is an upper bound.
- **The board bay** is not printed (Martin, 11/04: "For now, add a simple back mount and if there is space for the board to be screwed somewhere do it"): the back mount is the grammar's column, and the board lies on the palm's back where it finds room, which on most hands it does not (step 19). A bay at the column's far end (the design of 10/23) would give every hand's board a place.
- **The printed hand** (step 19) has not been printed yet: the fits (0.2 mm), the 2 mm walls and arms, and the horn screws' hold in PLA/PETG need a test print. Cables run outside the links on the finger's back; their routing past each joint is left to the builder.
- **Palm cuts between neighbouring bend-first fingers** are impossible: their housings are 7 mm apart, the seam needs 8.6.
- **Coupled joints and the policy:** they are tied and not controlled; whether they appear as tokens is open.
- **A physics grasp check (C5)** comes up again once the grammar work is done.
